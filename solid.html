Stoodis

Airframe

Stoodis is a 175 cm rocket with a 5.15-inch outer diameter, built to fly on an Aerotech K535W-14 motor and reach a projected apogee near 3,700 ft. The airframe combines 3D-printed structural components with fiberglass body tubing, chosen to balance strength, weight, and cost across the vehicle.

The nosecone follows a Von-Karman aerodynamic profile in CAD, though the team will manufacture it as a biconic shape since it closely approximates Von-Karman performance while being easier to produce. It's 3D-printed from PA612CF, a glass-fiber-reinforced polymer chosen over aluminum for its lower weight, cost-effectiveness, and stiffness under aerodynamic loading. The finished nosecone weighs 905g, with an additional 1000g of tunable mass built in to adjust flight performance depending on final weight or weather conditions on launch day.

The fin can, at the aft end, integrates the fins and houses the motor in an inner tube. It uses a tapered boat-tail design for structural support and aerodynamic control, with the fins slotted through the outer shell for reinforcement and a plate screwed into the internal structure to keep the fins seated during flight. The outer shell is 3D-printed PA12 glass fiber, while the fins and bottom plate are cut from 6061-T6 aluminum — chosen over wood (too heavy) and cardboard (too weak) for the best strength-to-weight balance.

The fin can, at the aft end, integrates the fins and houses the motor in an inner tube. It uses a Two bulkheads seal the CO2 canister volumes, secured by friction-fit and Nylon 4-40 shearing pins, capped with water-jetted T6 aluminum plates. Body tube sections are G12 fiberglass. Total vehicle mass, with motor, comes to 7,814g, with the center of gravity at 41.6" and center of pressure at 59.0" from the nosecone tip — giving a static stability margin of 3.38 calibers, comfortably within safe limits.

avionics

The avionics bay sits at the center of the rocket, epoxied to the inner radius of the body tube between the upper and lower recovery control volumes. It houses two independent Blue Raven flight altimeters arranged in a dual-modular-redundant (DMR) architecture, meaning either altimeter can independently trigger both parachute deployments if the other loses power or misreads the rocket's state.

Each altimeter fires two 12g Eagle CO2 canisters per parachute — one for the drogue at apogee, and one for the main at 700 ft — chosen for reliable, consistent ejection compared to traditional black-powder charges. An EggTimer GPS Mini transmitter, on its own dedicated pull-pin and power circuit, provides independent position tracking separate from the recovery electronics.

Arming is handled entirely through mechanical pull-pin switches rather than wireless or magnetic arming systems. The team specifically favored pull pins because they require no power or signal to function, give a visible physical indication of armed state, and eliminate failure modes tied to software, RF interference, or battery dropout — trading some convenience for reliability in a safety-critical system.

Structurally, an M10 threaded rod runs through the avionics bay, anchoring eyenuts on either end that connect via Kevlar cords to the parachutes and the bulkheads above and below. The bay's 3D-printed housing (PA6GF) weighs 427g, with electronics currently estimated at 200g pending final battery and CO2 canister weights.

The bay was originally planned for the nosecone but was relocated into the central body tube after the team found sufficient space there — simplifying wiring, switch placement, and integration while keeping it physically isolated from the separate challenge/payload electronics housed alongside it.
